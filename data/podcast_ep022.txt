ALEX: Before we get to our scenarios, let’s step back and learn something about how different parts of the market tend to take turns leading the way.
SAM: You mean like the way some stocks feel stronger while others feel weaker, depending on the bigger picture?
ALEX: Exactly. Think of the economy as a four‑season year. In the “spring” of growth, companies that sell consumer goods often shine because people have more cash to spend. In the “summer” of expanding production, industrial firms and materials tend to pull ahead as factories need more inputs. When the “autumn” of slower growth arrives, defensive sectors—utilities and consumer staples—usually hold up better. Finally, in the “winter” of a downturn, cash‑heavy businesses and those that benefit from lower rates can look more attractive.
SAM: So it’s not about a single stock doing well or badly, but about whole categories moving together?
ALEX: Right. The shift is driven by where investors expect earnings to grow or shrink, and by the cost of borrowing. When interest rates are low, growth‑oriented companies get a boost because financing is cheap. When rates rise, investors often rotate toward sectors that are less sensitive to financing costs.
SAM: But how do we know which season we’re in?
ALEX: You look at a handful of signals—like the pace of wage growth, the direction of central‑bank policy, and the level of corporate inventories. Those clues help you guess whether the economy is heating up, cooling down, or staying steady.

ALEX: For a high‑risk, growth‑focused portfolio, you might tilt a bit more into the “spring” and “summer” sectors when you think the economy is expanding, but keep a defensive cushion for the “autumn” and “winter” phases. That way, you capture upside while limiting the blow‑back if the cycle turns.
