[EDUCATION_TOPIC: Options Implied Volatility Basics]

ALEX: Before we get to our scenarios, let’s step back and learn something that often pops up in these conversations—what implied volatility actually is and why it matters, even if you don’t trade options.

SAM: I hear the term all the time when analysts talk about “IV crush” after earnings. Okay but what does that actually mean in practice?

ALEX: Implied volatility, or IV, is the market’s forecast of how much a stock’s price will move over the life of an option, expressed as an annualized percentage. Think of it as the premium you pay for insurance against price swings. When the market expects a big move—like an earnings surprise—it bids up the option price, inflating IV. After the event passes, the uncertainty disappears, and IV collapses, often wiping out a large chunk of the option’s value, regardless of whether the stock went up or down.

SAM: So it’s like a time‑decay factor baked into the option price?

ALEX: Exactly, but it’s separate from the standard theta decay. Theta measures the erosion of time value, while IV reflects the market’s expectation of volatility. If IV is high, the option is expensive; if IV drops, the option price falls even if the underlying stays flat. That’s why “IV crush” can be brutal for option sellers after a high‑IV event.

SAM: How does this tie into Christopher’s portfolio, with all that 3× leverage and a modest tech allocation?

ALEX: Two ways. First, the leveraged ETFs we hold are essentially a basket of futures and swaps, which embed volatility risk. When VIX stays above 22, as we’ve seen, the cost of maintaining those positions rises, eroding returns. Second, if you ever consider buying protective puts on Nvidia or Broadcom, you’ll pay a premium that’s heavily driven by IV. In a choppy market, that premium can be a sizable drain on your cash reserve, which we need to keep above $20,000 CAD for the FHSA timeline.

SAM: So monitoring IV gives us a sense of how expensive hedges are and whether the market’s pricing in more risk than we’re comfortable with.

ALEX: Precisely. Keeping an eye on the IV levels of the S&P 500 options chain can help us decide when to trim leveraged exposure or when to deploy cash into lower‑volatility assets like Broadcom’s dividend.
