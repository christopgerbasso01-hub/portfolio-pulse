[EDUCATION_TOPIC: Free Cash Flow, the Number Behind the Earnings]
ALEX: Free cash flow is the cash a company generates after it pays for the day‑to‑day operations and the money it spends to keep the business running, like buying equipment or maintaining factories.
SAM: So it's the cash that actually sits in the bank after all the bills are covered?
ALEX: Think of it as the water that makes it through a garden hose after the nozzle is turned on – the flow you can actually use to water the garden, not the water that's lost to leaks.
SAM: Walk me through the calculation, step by step.
ALEX: Start with operating cash flow, the cash from core business activities. Then subtract capital expenditures, the cash spent on long‑term assets. The remainder is free cash flow.
SAM: If a company reports operating cash flow of $150 and capex of $50, subtracting capex leaves $100 of free cash flow.
ALEX: That $100 is the amount the firm can allocate to pay down debt, buy back shares, invest in new projects, or simply add to its cash pile.
SAM: Why do analysts focus on that number instead of net income?
ALEX: Net income includes non‑cash items like depreciation and can be swayed by accounting choices. Free cash flow strips those away and shows the actual cash the business creates, which is harder to manipulate.
SAM: How does this help us evaluate a holding like Nvidia?
ALEX: Nvidia's free cash flow tells us how much cash it has left after funding its massive chip‑fab investments. If that cash is growing, it suggests the company can keep funding R&D, reward shareholders, and weather a slowdown without needing external financing.
SAM: And for a leveraged fund, say the S&P 500 3x, does free cash flow matter?
ALEX: Leveraged funds don't generate cash themselves, but the underlying stocks' free cash flow influences the fund's performance. Strong cash generation across the index supports earnings growth, which in turn helps the fund's price move with the market.

ALEX: We keep an eye on the big contributors – the tech giants, the semiconductor names, and the banks. If a company's free cash flow turns negative, it could signal a need for external capital, which might affect its price and, by extension, the weighted exposure we have.
