[EDUCATION_TOPIC: Short Interest Signals]  

ALEX: Before we get to our scenarios, let’s step back and learn something that’s never been in our show before—how **short interest works and what unusually high short interest can tell us**.  

SAM: Short interest… that’s the number of shares people have borrowed and sold, right?  

ALEX: Correct. It’s reported as a raw share count and also as a percentage of the float, which is the number of shares actually available for trading. When the short‑interest ratio climbs above, say, **15 % of float**, it signals that a sizable chunk of investors expect the price to drop.  

SAM: So a high short‑interest number is just a bearish bet?  

ALEX: It’s more nuanced. A large short position creates the potential for a **short squeeze**. If the stock price starts rising, short sellers may be forced to cover—buy the stock back—to limit losses. That buying pressure can accelerate the price move, turning a modest rally into a sharp spike.  

SAM: How does that play out in practice for a portfolio like Christopher’s?  

ALEX: Imagine we own a stock with a 20 % short‑interest ratio, and the price jumps 10 % in a day. Short sellers might scramble to cover, adding another 5 % of buying pressure. That secondary boost could translate into an extra **$2,500 CAD** gain on a $50,000 position. Conversely, if the stock falls and short interest stays high, the downside can be deeper because the market sentiment is already skewed bearish.  

SAM: So it’s a double‑edged sword—potential upside from a squeeze, but also a warning sign of downside risk.  

ALEX: Exactly. For a high‑risk investor with leverage, monitoring short interest helps you gauge where a stock could erupt or crumble. You can use it to time entries, set tighter stops, or even add a small hedge if you think a squeeze is imminent.  

SAM: Okay, that makes sense. It’s a metric that adds context beyond just price trends.
