ALEX: Before we get to our scenarios, let’s step back and learn something about **yield‑curve recession signals**.
SAM: Yield curve? I know it’s the line connecting Treasury yields, but why does its shape matter to us?
ALEX: The **yield curve** plots the interest rates of government bonds across different maturities. In a healthy, growing economy, **long‑term yields** are higher than **short‑term yields** because investors demand extra compensation for the time risk – that’s a **normal upward‑sloping curve**. When investors start expecting slower growth or a possible recession, they demand **less premium** for holding longer‑term debt, and the curve can **flatten** or even **invert** (short‑term rates exceed long‑term rates). Historically, an **inverted 2‑year/10‑year spread** has preceded many U.S. recessions by **6‑18 months**.
SAM: Okay, but how do we translate that into a concrete action for our portfolio?
ALEX: Think of the **curve as a leading indicator** for the **equity market**. When the curve flattens, it often signals that **corporate earnings growth will stall**, which drags down stock prices, especially **growth‑oriented sectors** like tech. In our portfolio, the **leveraged ETFs** (FNGU, SPXL, Dow 3×) are **high‑beta** to the market. A **10‑basis‑point flattening** of the 2‑year/10‑year spread historically correlates with a **0.3 % drop** in the **S&P 500** over the next month. That **0.3 % dip** would shave **$4,931 CAD × 0.3 ≈ $1,479 CAD** off our leveraged exposure. Conversely, if the spread **steepens** by 10 bps, we could see a **0.3 % gain**, adding roughly the same amount.
SAM: So we need to watch the spread, but what’s the trigger point? Do we have a specific number?
ALEX: The **key level** is when the **2‑year yield exceeds the 10‑year yield**. Right now, the **2‑year is at 4.78 %**, the **10‑year at 4.85 %** – a **7‑basis‑point gap**. If the **2‑year climbs to 5.00 %** while the **10‑year stays below 5.00 %**, we’ll have a **full inversion**, which historically has been a **strong recession signal**. We’ll keep an eye on the **Federal Reserve’s policy meetings** – the next one is **October 27** – because any hawkish stance could push short‑term rates higher and trigger that inversion.
