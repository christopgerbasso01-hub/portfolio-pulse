SAM: So what exactly is a yield‑curve signal?
ALEX: It’s the shape formed by bond yields across different maturities, plotted like a hill or a valley.
SAM: And why does the shape matter?
ALEX: When short‑term rates climb above long‑term rates, the curve inverts, meaning investors expect weaker growth ahead.
SAM: Is an inversion always a bad sign?
ALEX: Historically, a sustained inversion has preceded most recessions, but it’s a warning, not a guarantee.
SAM: How do we see that in numbers?
ALEX: The short rate sits higher, flipping the curve.
SAM: Got it. What’s the step‑by‑step process to read it?
ALEX: First, pick two benchmark maturities—commonly three months and two years, or two years and ten years.
SAM: Then we compare their yields?
ALEX: Exactly. If the shorter‑term yield exceeds the longer‑term yield, you have an inversion at that spread.
SAM: Do we track just one spread?
ALEX: You can watch several spreads; consistency across them strengthens the signal.
ALEX: Second, monitor how long the inversion lasts. A brief dip may be noise; a weeks‑long inversion carries more weight.
SAM: How long is “weeks‑long”?
ALEX: Typically, if the inversion persists for more than two weeks, analysts start paying attention.
SAM: And the third step?
ALEX: Compare the current spread to historical averages. A larger gap than usual suggests stronger recession expectations.
SAM: So the larger the gap, the more serious?
ALEX: In general, yes. A wide inversion signals that market participants price future growth as significantly lower.

ALEX: One practical use is to adjust the portfolio’s duration—shortening exposure to longer‑term bonds if you anticipate a downturn.
SAM: Shortening duration means what, exactly?
ALEX: It means shifting weight toward bonds that mature sooner, reducing sensitivity to falling rates that often accompany recessions.
