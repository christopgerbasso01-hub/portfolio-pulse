ALEX: The yield curve plots interest rates on bonds of different maturities, typically from three months up to thirty years.
SAM: So it’s just a graph of short‑ and long‑term rates?
ALEX: Exactly, and the shape tells us about market expectations. When short‑term rates sit above long‑term rates, the curve inverts—a classic recession warning.
SAM: Why would investors demand higher yields on short‑term debt than on long‑term?
ALEX: They’re pricing in tighter monetary policy and slower growth ahead, so they want extra compensation now.
SAM: How do we spot that inversion in practice?
ALEX: First, pick two benchmark bonds—say, the three‑month and ten‑year. Compare their yields. If the three‑month exceeds the ten‑year, you have an inversion.
SAM: Got it. Does the depth matter?
ALEX: The bigger the spread, the stronger the signal. A modest flip might be noise; a wide gap has historically preceded recessions.
SAM: And what about a flat curve?
SAM: AALIGN: A flat curve suggests uncertainty—neither side dominates. It’s a neutral zone, not a trigger.
SAM: How would you turn that into an investment move?
ALEX: You could tilt toward assets that tend to hold up in downturns—like high‑quality dividend stocks, defensive sectors, or cash‑like holdings. Conversely, you might trim exposure to cyclical growth names that are vulnerable.
